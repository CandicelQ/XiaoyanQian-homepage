---
title: "Most of the time it is right, and that is the problem: why a patient-facing medical AI should be judged by what it refuses"
date: 2026-09-02
summary: "Medical AI is evaluated almost entirely on whether its answers are correct. That metric is silent on the failure that actually reaches patients, which is not a wrong answer but a fluent and helpful answer to a question the system had no standing to answer at all. This note sets out the position I am building against: that refusal, escalation and the durability of the record are not safety wrapping around a clinical system but part of the system boundary, and that a refusal is only worth anything if it leaves evidence a clinician can open months later. It also states plainly what makes this hard, which is not the idea but the taxonomy, the thresholds, the schema and the clinical access underneath it."
---

Between two clinic visits, a patient with a chronic condition is alone with their questions for something close to eight thousand hours a year. Increasingly they put those questions to a general-purpose assistant, and increasingly they get a good answer. That last part is not a rhetorical concession before the real argument. It is the argument.

**The way medical AI is currently evaluated is silent on the failure that actually reaches patients.** Benchmarks ask whether the answer was correct. Almost none ask whether the system should have answered at all. Those are different questions, and the second one is where harm lives, because a patient cannot audit correctness and does not try to. What a patient can perceive is fluency, confidence and helpfulness, and a system that is usually right has taught them that fluency is a reliable proxy. It usually is. That is precisely what makes the exception undetectable from inside the conversation.

Consider the shape of it. A patient asks a question that is squarely in scope: my fasting glucose has drifted up, what is going on. A well-built system answers it well, with a range and a caveat. Then, in the same breath and with no change in register, the patient asks the next question, which is not in scope: should I halve my metformin, do I have kidney damage. **Nothing in the interface marks the boundary that was just crossed, because the boundary is not in the interface. It is in the clinical standing of the answer, and the system has no way to show it.** An assistant that replies to the second question in the same reassuring tone as the first has not malfunctioned by any measure it is being scored on. It has been helpful.

If such systems were usually wrong we would have stopped using them. The reason this is a governance problem rather than a model-quality problem is that the base rate of correctness is high and rising, and every increment in that base rate increases the authority of the exceptions.

**So the claim I am working from is that a patient-facing medical AI should be judged primarily by what it declines to do, and that a decline is worth nothing unless it leaves a record.** Three things follow from that, and I want to state them as commitments rather than features, because features get descoped and commitments have to be argued away.

*Refusal is a clinical act, not an error state.* When a system withholds, it is exercising a judgement about the limits of its own authority, and that judgement has to be legible: what it will not do, what it can still do, what the patient can usefully observe in the meantime, and where the question now sits. A refusal that only says no has transferred the risk back to the least-equipped person in the exchange. A patient who is told nothing more than "consult your doctor" and whose appointment is nine days away has been abandoned politely.

*Escalation must be a benefit, not an inbox.* The standing objection to routing anything to a human is that clinicians are already saturated, and the objection is correct as usually implemented. It stops being correct only if the escalation arrives as preparation, so that the consultation starts further along than it otherwise would have. An escalation that costs a clinician time is a governance mechanism that will be switched off within a quarter, and any design that does not confront that is not serious.

*The record must outlive the conversation.* This is the part I care most about and the part that is most often waved at. Months after the fact, someone reviewing an adverse outcome needs to be able to open the exact exchange where the system stopped, see what it declined to say, and see that the decline was routed to a person, unaltered. **A refusal that is not reconstructible is indistinguishable from a refusal that never happened.** This is where governance becomes an engineering constraint rather than a policy statement, because durability, provenance and version-binding have to be designed in from the first commit or they cannot be added later without rewriting what the record means.

None of this is exotic, and I want to be clear that the position is not what is hard here. Versions of this argument are in the literature already. What is hard is everything underneath it: a refusal taxonomy that a clinician will sign rather than tolerate; thresholds specifying which presentations trigger which tier of escalation, which cannot be derived from first principles and have to come out of clinical judgement; an audit schema that binds each output to the model, prompt, rule and configuration versions that produced it; an evaluation harness that scores withholding rather than answering, which means building the negative test set that nobody has an incentive to build; and, hardest of all, the clinical and ethical standing to work with real patient data rather than synthetic scenarios. Those are the things that determine whether any of this becomes real, and none of them are shortened by having the idea.

I am building this as the Medical Digital Twin Initiative, begun in August 2026. It is pre-deployment and it works on synthetic scenarios; there are no clinical results to report and no claim that any of it has been validated on patients. What exists is a governance record and a working demonstration of the interaction pattern, and I would rather state the position now, with a date on it, than wait until there is something impressive enough to be safe.

A short film accompanies this note. It follows one patient across a single question that should have been refused, and shows the same moment twice: once with no governance, and once with it, including what the log looks like three months later. It is an illustrative synthetic scenario depicting a documented category of harm, not a specific product and not a real case, and it deliberately shows only what the system does, not how it decides. The point it ends on is the one this note began with.

Most of the time, it is right. That is the problem.
